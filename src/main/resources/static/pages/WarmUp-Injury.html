Warm-Up and Injury Prevention

Learn how proper preparation, exercise technique, and sensible training progression can help improve performance and reduce unnecessary injury risk.

Why Warm-Up Matters

A proper warm-up prepares the body for physical activity by gradually increasing core body temperature and blood flow to working muscles. This process helps to:

  • check Increase joint viscosity and muscle elasticity.
  • check Gradually elevate heart rate and respiratory rate.
  • check Prime the central nervous system for complex movements.
  • check Provide mental preparation and focus for the session ahead.

General vs Specific

General Warm-Up

5-10 minutes of light aerobic activity (walking, cycling, rowing) to elevate core temperature without causing significant fatigue.

Specific Warm-Up

Movement patterns that mimic the upcoming exercises. This includes dynamic stretching and empty bar work (e.g., performing a bench press with only the 20kg bar before adding weight).

Warm-Up Sets: Ramping Up

Warm-up sets are essential for bridging the gap between an empty bar and your target working weight. They allow you to practice technique, gauge daily readiness, and prepare tissues for heavy loading without accumulating excessive fatigue.

Example: Working toward 80kg for 8 reps

Set 1 (Empty) 20kg x10
Set 2 40kg x8
Set 3 60kg x3-5
Working Set 80kg x8

Mobility vs Flexibility

settings_accessibility Mobility

Active control over a joint's range of motion. Strength applied through a full range. Highly relevant for lifting safely.

airline_seat_recline_extra Flexibility

Passive length of a muscle. While useful, passive flexibility without active control (mobility) does not protect joints under load.

Proper Technique

Good technique is the primary defense against training injuries. It involves:

  • arrow_right Maintaining control of the load at all times (especially the eccentric phase).
  • arrow_right Utilizing a full, comfortable range of motion specific to your individual anatomy.
  • arrow_right Ensuring stability (e.g., bracing the core) to transfer force effectively.
monitoring

Load Management

The most common cause of training-related injuries is doing too much, too soon. Load management refers to the sensible planning and progression of your training volume and intensity.

Avoid sudden spikes in training stress. Progression should be gradual and sustainable, monitoring fatigue levels and allowing for adequate recovery between demanding sessions.
